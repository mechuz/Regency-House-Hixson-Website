The week before Thanksgiving, the same quiet worry shows up in a lot of Chattanooga households. Mom is coming for dinner, or you are driving to Dad's house, or a brother is flying in from Nashville with the kids, and somewhere between the grocery list and the guest beds you realize the holiday is going to look different this year. A parent who used to run the kitchen now tires by two in the afternoon. The stairs at your house are a problem. Nobody has said out loud who is going to make sure the pills get taken on the right day.

This guide is for the adult son or daughter who is planning the holidays around an aging parent for the first time, or who did it last year and swore it would go better this time. It covers the visit itself, travel in either direction, how to keep the main caregiver from running on empty, and when a short respite stay makes the whole season easier for everyone.

This article is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice.

Start with a smaller plan than you think you need

The Alzheimer's Association's holiday guidance, written for families of people with dementia but sound for any older parent, opens with the same advice every experienced caregiver eventually gives: scale back. Nobody is obliged to keep every tradition alive in the same year a parent's energy, hearing or mobility has changed. A shorter gathering, an earlier mealtime and fewer guests at once are not a failure. They are how you make sure the person the day is supposed to honor actually enjoys it.

Three adjustments do most of the work:

  • Move the meal earlier. Many older adults are at their best late morning and early afternoon and fade by evening. A midday dinner with a clear end time lets a parent be present for the part that matters and home, or resting, before the late-evening confusion and fatigue that families often notice.
  • Give them a quiet room. A bedroom or den with the door mostly closed, a comfortable chair and a glass of water is the single most useful thing a host can set aside. Twenty minutes away from a loud table is often the difference between a good day and an early exit.
  • Give them a job. Folding napkins, snapping beans, reading the blessing, telling the grandchildren the story behind a dish. Being useful is worth more than being waited on.

If a parent has memory loss, the Association also suggests sending relatives a short note ahead of time explaining what has changed and how to greet them, and keeping decorations simple: blinking lights and food-shaped ornaments can confuse someone with dementia. Families who need a secure memory care setting should look for a community specifically licensed for that; standard assisted living, including ours, is not designed for it.

Traveling with a parent, or bringing a parent to you

Holiday travel is where good intentions meet long security lines. Whether Dad is flying to you or you are driving him across town, a little structure prevents most of the trouble.

Before anyone leaves the house

AARP's guide to traveling with an older parent recommends a doctor's visit or at least a phone call before any trip of more than a day or two, to ask about refills, travel precautions and anything in the parent's history that makes a long drive or a flight unwise. Out of that conversation, build one page that travels with the parent: every medication with dose and timing, allergies, diagnoses, the doctor's phone number, and the name of the person to call first. Keep a photo of it on your phone and a paper copy in their bag.

Pack medications in the carry-on, never in checked luggage, in their original labeled containers. Bring a few extra days' supply in case a return trip slips.

At the airport

Request wheelchair assistance when you book, not at the counter. Under federal rules airlines must provide it to and from the gate, between connecting gates and to baggage claim; AARP notes it is easiest to arrange ahead by phone and that most airlines will allow an older traveler and a companion to board early if asked. Even a parent who walks fine at home may be grateful for a ride through a terminal the size of a small town.

Security has its own rules, and knowing them in advance keeps the line from becoming a crisis. The TSA Cares program lets you call 72 hours before a flight to arrange help through the checkpoint for a traveler with a disability or medical condition. Passengers 75 and older who use a wheelchair or scooter can stay seated for screening. TSA's medication rules allow medically necessary liquids over the usual 3.4-ounce limit, as long as you declare them at the start of screening, and you can ask for a visual inspection instead of X-ray if you prefer. A note from the doctor is not required but shortens the conversation.

On the road

For a drive to a daughter's house in Ooltewah or a son's place in Knoxville, AARP's advice is simply to build in more stops than you think you need. Getting out to walk every hour or two reduces stiffness and the risk of blood clots on longer trips, and it gives a parent with a weaker bladder a predictable rhythm instead of an anxious one. Travel in daylight when you can, bring a blanket and water, and do not plan anything else for the arrival day.

Keeping everyone healthy through the season

The holidays are also the start of respiratory season, and a crowded living room is where a lot of winter illness in older adults begins. Two things are worth settling before the gatherings start.

First, vaccines. The CDC recommends that adults 65 and older get one of three enhanced flu vaccines rather than a standard-dose shot, because the immune response to the standard shot weakens with age; if none of the three is available, any flu vaccine is better than none, and it is not too late in November. The CDC's RSV guidance recommends a single lifetime dose for everyone 75 and older and for people 50 to 74 at higher risk. Ask the pharmacy or the parent's doctor; both shots can usually be done in one visit.

Second, the house rules. The CDC's holiday health tips are not complicated: wash hands with soap for at least 20 seconds, handle food safely, and keep anyone who is sick away from the gathering. For an older parent, the hard part is the last one. A grandchild with a cough is still a grandchild. The kindest version is a video call from the next room and a promise of a makeup visit in January.

The caregiver needs a holiday too

If one person in your family is the primary caregiver, the holidays usually make their job harder, not easier. More people in the house, more meals, more driving, and the same medication schedule underneath all of it. The Alzheimer's Association's advice to relatives is direct: offer to host instead of letting the caregiver host, take over the cooking or the shopping, and give the gift that matters most, which is scheduled, dependable time off.

That last point is where many families discover respite care. The National Institute on Aging describes respite as short-term care for an older adult, at home, in a facility or at an adult day program, lasting anywhere from a few hours to several weeks, so the regular caregiver can rest, travel or simply be a guest at their own table for once. In practice, around the holidays it looks like one of these:

  • A parent stays in a furnished respite apartment at an assisted living community for the week a daughter travels to see her in-laws, with meals, medication help and staff on duty overnight.
  • A parent who lives with a son spends a few days in respite care over Christmas so the son's family can have one holiday that is not organized around care.
  • A parent recovering from a fall or a hospital stay spends two or three weeks in respite rather than going home to an empty house while the family is traveling.

A simple holiday plan, written down

Families who get through the season well tend to have agreed on a few things in advance, usually in a group text in early November. It is not too late to do it now:

  1. Who is the point person for Mom or Dad on each day? One name per day, so nobody assumes someone else is watching the medication clock.
  2. What time does the main event start and end? Put the end time in writing. It gives the parent permission to leave and the host permission to stop.
  3. Where is the quiet room, and who checks on it?
  4. What is the travel plan, door to door? Including who carries the medication bag and the one-page health summary.
  5. Who is giving the caregiver a day off, and which day? A specific date, not a vague offer.
  6. What is the plan if someone in the house gets sick? Decide now, when nobody is upset.

What to notice while you are together

A long visit also shows you things a phone call never will. You do not need to turn Thanksgiving into an inspection, but the signs AARP lists are worth a quiet look: expired food in the refrigerator, mail and bills piling up, new dents on the car, a prescription that was never refilled, weight loss, or a parent who has stopped seeing friends. If you notice several, the right next step is usually a conversation with the parent's doctor after the holiday, not a confrontation at the table.
