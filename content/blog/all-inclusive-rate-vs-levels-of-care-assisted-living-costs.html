Call three assisted living communities in the Chattanooga area and ask the same question, "What does it cost?", and you may get three answers that cannot be laid side by side. One quotes a single monthly rate. Another quotes a base rent and then a "level of care" that will be set after an assessment. The three ways assisted living is usually priced

Most assisted living communities in the United States price their services in one of three ways. Consumer guides such as the one from Seniorly's resource center describe the same three patterns, though the names vary from company to company.

1. All-inclusive

One monthly rate covers the apartment and the care and services the community provides: meals, housekeeping, laundry, activities, help with bathing, dressing and other daily routines, medication reminders or assistance, and 24-hour staff presence. The rate is usually set by the apartment a resident chooses, so a larger apartment costs more than a studio, but the care portion does not move up and down with the resident's needs.

2. Levels of care

The community charges a base rate for the apartment, meals and basic services, then adds a care charge based on an assessment. Most communities that use this model have three to five tiers. A resident who needs only a little help pays the lowest tier. A resident who needs help with several daily activities, or who needs more frequent checks, moves into a higher tier at a higher monthly charge. Residents are reassessed periodically, and the tier, and the bill, can change after each reassessment.

3. À la carte or points-based

The community charges a base rate and then prices individual services separately. Medication management, escorts to meals, incontinence care, extra housekeeping or a second person for transfers each carry their own fee. Some communities translate the services into points and charge by points per month. The monthly total is the base rate plus whatever the resident actually used.

None of these models is dishonest and none is always cheaper. They shift the risk of changing needs in different directions, and that is the part families need to understand.

What "all-inclusive" really promises, and what it does not

An all-inclusive rate promises predictability. If your mother moves in needing a little help with her morning routine and a year later needs help with bathing, dressing and medications, her monthly bill under a true all-inclusive model does not change because of that. The community has priced the care portion for the average resident across the building rather than for each person individually.

That predictability is the main reason families choose the model. It makes a multi-year budget possible, it removes the dread of the reassessment letter, and it means a resident whose needs grow is not quietly priced out of the home they have settled into.

It is worth being precise about what the promise does not cover, because the words "all-inclusive" are used loosely across the industry:

  • Annual increases. An all-inclusive rate is still a rate, and most communities adjust rates once a year for wages, food and utilities. Inclusive means the price does not change because your parent's needs changed. It does not mean the price never changes.
  • Services outside the community's scope. Assisted living is not skilled nursing. If a resident needs care a licensed assisted care living facility is not permitted to provide, that care comes from an outside provider, such as a home health or hospice agency, and is billed by that provider under its own rules.
  • Personal items. Telephone, cable, salon services, personal supplies and incontinence products are commonly billed separately or bought by the family, even under inclusive pricing. Ask.
  • A community fee or deposit. Many communities charge a one-time fee at move-in. It is separate from the monthly rate under every model.

Some communities advertise "all-inclusive" and then quietly add a care tier on top once a resident's needs cross a threshold. That is a levels-of-care model wearing a different label. The test is simple: ask whether the monthly rate for a given apartment can change for any reason other than the annual increase. If the honest answer is yes, you are looking at tiered pricing.

Why care-level pricing is not the villain either

Families sometimes hear "levels of care" and assume they are being set up. That is not fair to the communities that use the model well. For a resident who genuinely needs very little help and is likely to stay that way, a low base rate plus the lowest tier can be less expensive than an inclusive rate, at least for a while. The model also lets a community serve a wider range of needs under one roof, because the residents who need the most care pay for the most care.

The weakness of the model is that it puts the risk of change on the family. Needs in assisted living rarely go down. A resident who moves in at the lowest tier will, on average, move up over time, and each step up is a new monthly figure. Families who budgeted on the move-in quote often find the real cost two years later is substantially higher, not because anyone misled them but because nobody walked them through where the tiers lead.

If you are considering a community that prices by level, the right questions are: How many tiers are there? What is the monthly charge at each one? How often is my parent reassessed, and who decides? What happens at the top tier? If the person doing the tour cannot give you the full tier schedule in writing, that is a signal.

What the market looks like right now

It helps to know the national backdrop before you judge any single quote. CareScout's 2025 Cost of Care Survey, released in March 2026, puts the national median cost of assisted living at about $6,200 a month, or roughly $74,400 a year, up about 5 percent from the year before. That figure is a median across every pricing model and every state, so a specific community in Hamilton County may sit above or below it for perfectly good reasons. CareScout publishes state and metro-area figures as well, and the Tennessee numbers are worth a look when you start comparing quotes.

The important thing about a median is that it is a snapshot of what people are paying today, including residents whose tiered rates have already climbed. A move-in quote under a levels-of-care model is almost always below what the same resident pays two years later. An all-inclusive quote is closer to the real long-run number from the start.

A fair way to compare two communities with different models

Brochures cannot be compared. Scenarios can. Use this method with every community on your list.

  1. Pick a realistic apartment. Decide what size your parent needs, a studio or a one-bedroom for example, and ask each community for the monthly rate on that apartment specifically, not the "starting at" figure for the smallest unit in the building.
  2. Describe today's needs honestly. Tell each community what help your parent needs right now: medication reminders, help with a shower twice a week, a walker, whatever is true. Ask what the total monthly cost would be today under their model.
  3. Describe a likely future. Then ask: if in eighteen months my parent needs daily help with bathing and dressing, medication management and an escort to meals, what would the total be? An inclusive community will give you the same number. A tiered community should be able to name the tier and the charge.
  4. Ask for everything that is not in the monthly rate. Community fee, deposit, second-person fee, incontinence supplies, cable, phone, transportation beyond the scheduled outings, pet fee, and the charge if a resident needs a short hospital stay and holds the apartment.
  5. Ask about the annual increase. When does it happen, how much notice is given, and what has it been in recent years?
  6. Get it in writing. Every community licensed as an assisted care living facility in Tennessee must give residents a written admission agreement; the rules are published by the Tennessee Health Facilities Commission. Before you sign, read the sections on charges, rate changes and the circumstances under which a resident may be asked to move, and make sure every fee you were quoted appears in the document.

Write the answers to steps two and three on one sheet, today's total and the eighteen-month total for each community. That sheet is the comparison. It will often show that a community whose first quote looked higher is the less expensive one over any realistic stay.

Questions worth asking on every tour

  • Can the monthly rate for this apartment change for any reason other than the annual adjustment?
  • If you use levels of care, may I see the full schedule of tiers and charges?
  • Who performs the care assessment, how often, and can the family be present?
  • What would cause a resident to be asked to move, and how much notice is given?
  • What is included in the rate for housekeeping, laundry and meals, and what is extra?
  • Is there a community fee or deposit, and is any of it refundable?
  • If my parent is in the hospital for a week, what do we pay for that week?
