The call usually comes in the afternoon. Your mother has been in the hospital for three days after a fall, and the nurse says the doctors expect to release her soon. Then comes the question nobody prepared you for: where is she going next? Home, where the stairs and the pill organizer were already a worry? A short rehabilitation stay? Or somewhere with more day-to-day support, like assisted living?

If you are in that spot right now, take a breath. A hospital discharge feels like a deadline, but it is also a planning process with real people whose job is to help you, and you have more say in it than most families realize. This guide walks through what to ask, what Medicare does and does not cover, and how assisted living or a short respite stay can fit into the picture for families in Hixson and across Chattanooga.

This article is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice.

Start with the discharge planner, early

Every hospital has a discharge planner, often a nurse or social worker, whose job is to arrange what happens after a patient leaves. The Eldercare Locator's hospital discharge fact sheet from the Administration for Community Living advises patients and caregivers to talk with the discharge planner and to say plainly which care tasks they cannot manage at home, so that help can be arranged before the day of discharge.

Do not wait for the planner to find you. Ask the bedside nurse on day one, "Who is the discharge planner, and when can I meet them?" Medicare's own discharge planning checklist says the patient and caregiver are important members of the planning team. You are not interrupting. You are part of the process.

Questions worth writing down before you meet

The Medicare checklist and AARP's hospital discharge tips for older adults point to the same practical list. Bring a notebook or use your phone, and ask:

  • Where will my parent get care after leaving, and who recommends that setting?
  • Which of the medicines taken before admission should continue, and which are new or changed?
  • What warning signs should we watch for, and who do we call if they appear?
  • Will equipment such as a walker be needed, who orders it, and who arranges delivery?
  • Are there tasks, such as changing a bandage, that the staff can show me how to do before we leave?
  • What follow-up appointments are needed, and can they be scheduled before discharge?
  • Can we have the discharge instructions in writing, in plain language?
  • Who is the one person we can call with questions after we leave?

AARP also suggests asking whether you can record complicated instructions, with permission, so that you do not have to rely on memory during a stressful afternoon.

Know your discharge rights

One thing many families do not know: if your parent is a Medicare patient and you believe they are being sent home too soon, you can ask for an independent review. Medicare.gov explains that hospital patients should receive a notice called "An Important Message from Medicare about Your Rights" within two days of admission and again before discharge. If you have not received it, ask for it.

That notice names the Quality Improvement Organization, an independent reviewer, and gives a phone number for a fast appeal. According to Medicare.gov, the deadline is short: an appeal generally must be made by midnight of the day of discharge. Medicare also says these rights apply under Original Medicare and Medicare Advantage plans alike. You may never need this, but knowing it exists can change how confident you feel in the room.

What Medicare does and does not pay for after a hospital stay

This is the part that surprises families most, so it is worth reading slowly.

Medicare can help pay for a short stay in a skilled nursing facility, but only in specific circumstances. Medicare.gov says Original Medicare generally requires a qualifying inpatient hospital stay of at least three days in a row, and the care in the facility must be skilled care that a doctor orders. Some Medicare Advantage plans and some accountable care organizations handle that three-day rule differently, so ask the discharge planner and the plan itself. Time in the hospital "under observation" may not count as inpatient time, which is another good question to ask early.

Skilled nursing coverage is short-term and tied to a medical need for skilled care. It is not designed to pay for ongoing help with daily living, and Medicare does not pay for assisted living. If you are comparing options, a conversation with a State Health Insurance Assistance Program counselor or your parent's plan can clarify what their own coverage will and will not do. Always confirm details with Medicare, your parent's plan or a benefits counselor, because rules and dollar amounts change every year.

Where could your parent go from the hospital?

Families often assume there are only two choices, home or a nursing facility. In practice there are usually several, and the right answer depends on what the doctors say your parent needs.

Home with help

Some patients return home with home health visits arranged by the hospital, such as a nurse or therapist. This works best when the home is safe, someone can be there for the first days and the medication routine is clear. Ask the care team whether home health is recommended and who will set it up.

A short skilled or rehabilitation stay

If the doctors say your parent needs skilled nursing or therapy for a period of time, the discharge planner will talk with you about facilities that can provide it. Regency House is an assisted living community and does not provide skilled nursing, so that is a different kind of setting. If skilled care is what the doctors order, that is the first priority, and it is the kind of decision to make with your parent's medical team.

Assisted living or a respite stay

Sometimes a hospital stay shows what the family already suspected: Mom is managing, but only barely. Meals have been skipped, the medicines are hard to keep straight and the house has hazards. For a parent who does not need skilled nursing but does need more daily support than home can offer, assisted living may be a good next step.

Regency House in Hixson offers private apartments with meals, housekeeping, activities, medication help and staff on duty around the clock. For families who are not ready to decide, a respite stay in a furnished apartment can provide a short, low-pressure way to see what community living is like while the family catches its breath. Whether a stay is a fit for your parent depends on their needs after discharge, so it helps to talk it through with us and with their doctors before anyone moves.

How to tell whether assisted living is the right fit

Whether the move is permanent or a trial, these questions can keep the decision grounded:

  1. What do the doctors say my parent needs right now? Get it in writing, and make sure the next setting can provide it.
  2. What did a typical day look like before the hospital? If help was already stretched thin, a return to the same arrangement may not hold.
  3. What does my parent want? Their voice matters even when the timeline is short. The National Institute on Aging's caregiving resources are a good place to start for conversations about difficult decisions.
  4. Who will be there on day three, not just day one? Plans often fall apart after the first excitement and relief fade.
  5. How will we pay? Ask any community, ours included, how its rate works and what it covers, so there are no surprises.

A calm plan for the next 48 hours

  1. Ask for the discharge planner and schedule a conversation, not a hallway chat.
  2. Get the medication list, the follow-up appointments and the warning signs in writing.
  3. Ask whether your parent has been admitted as an inpatient or is under observation.
  4. Confirm whether a skilled stay is recommended and what your parent's plan says about it.
  5. Make a short list of the support your parent needs for the first week, and who can provide each piece.
  6. If assisted living is on the table, ask for a tour or a phone call before the discharge date, not after.
