Choosing Assisted Living in Hixson: A Family Checklist

If you are reading this late at night on your phone, you are not alone.

November 13, 2026 · Regency House Assisted Living · 7 min read

If you are reading this late at night on your phone, you are not alone. Most families start looking at assisted living after something happens: a fall, a hospital stay, a refrigerator that keeps turning up empty. The pressure to decide quickly is real. This checklist is meant to slow the decision down just enough to get it right, without turning it into a research project.

It is written for Hixson, Chattanooga and the rest of Hamilton County, but the method works anywhere.

Start with what your parent actually needs

Assisted living is for someone who needs help with daily living, such as bathing, dressing, getting to meals or keeping track of medications, but who does not need round-the-clock medical treatment. If your parent needs skilled nursing care every day, or has dementia that makes wandering a safety risk, a different setting is the right answer, and a good community will tell you so on the first call.

Before you tour anything, write down three things:

  • What your parent needs help with today. Be specific. "Forgets the evening pills" is more useful than "needs some help."
  • What has changed in the last six months. A pattern of falls, weight loss or missed appointments tells you the direction things are heading.
  • What matters to your parent. A garden, a church group, a familiar neighborhood, a cat. These decide whether a move works, not just whether it is safe.

Check the license first

In Tennessee, an assisted living community is licensed by the state as an Assisted Care Living Facility. Ask every community you consider for its current license, and if memory care matters to you, ask whether the community is approved by the state to operate a secure unit for residents with dementia. Tennessee does not issue a separate memory care license; a locked unit runs under the assisted care living license with extra requirements, and a community that has not been approved for one cannot operate it, no matter what its brochure implies. The Tennessee Health Facilities Commission publishes provider information and is the office to call if you want to confirm a license or ask about inspection history.

This is also the moment to ask the plain question: do you offer memory care? Some communities do. Regency House does not, and says so. A clear no is more useful to you than a vague yes.

Understand how the community charges

Pricing is where families get surprised. Many communities quote a base rent, then add a "level of care" charge after a nursing assessment, then add fees for medication management, incontinence care or extra help at meals. The number on the brochure and the number on the first invoice can be far apart. The National Consumer Voice for Quality Long-Term Care recommends asking for the full fee schedule in writing and asking exactly what triggers a move to a higher level.

Other communities, including Regency House, use one inclusive monthly rate set by the apartment, with care, meals, housekeeping, laundry and activities included and no care levels. Neither model is wrong, but you cannot compare them on the headline rent alone. Compare the total expected monthly cost for your parent's actual needs, this year and next.

For context, CareScout's 2025 Cost of Care Survey put the Tennessee median for a private one-bedroom assisted living apartment at about $5,845 a month, against a national median of $6,200. Individual communities range well above and below those figures.

Ask how the community is paid

Most assisted living in Tennessee is paid privately, from income, savings, a pension or the sale of a home. Some communities also participate in TennCare's CHOICES program or accept long-term care insurance assignments; others, including Regency House, are private pay only. Ask directly, and if you expect to rely on long-term care insurance or a veterans benefit, talk with a benefits counselor before you sign anything. This article is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice.

Tour at the right time, and tour twice

A scheduled tour at 10 in the morning shows you a building at its best. Ask to come back unannounced around a mealtime or in the early evening. AARP's guidance on assisted living makes the point that the person who gives the tour is usually a salesperson, so plan to look past the presentation.

On the tour, pay attention to:

  • How staff greet residents. Do they know names? Do residents look comfortable with them?
  • How long a call light goes unanswered. Ask the director for the average response time, then watch.
  • The dining room at a meal. Is the food fresh? Are residents eating together or alone in their rooms?
  • Smell, light and noise in the hallways. These tell you more about housekeeping than any checklist answer.
  • The activity calendar on the wall, and whether the activity listed for right now is actually happening.
  • The apartments. Private bathroom with grab bars and a walk-in shower? A kitchenette? Room for your parent's own furniture?

Ask about staffing, specifically

AARP suggests asking for the staff-to-resident ratio during the day, at night and on weekends, and asking how the community handles call-outs and shortages. Also ask:

  • Is a nurse on site, and during what hours?
  • Who administers medications, and how are they stored and recorded?
  • How long has the administrator been in the role? How long has the average caregiver been there?
  • What training does care staff receive, and how often?

Turnover is normal in this field, but a community that cannot answer these questions without checking is telling you something.

Read the contract before you love the building

Ask for the resident agreement in advance and read it at home. Look for the notice period if your parent needs to leave, the conditions under which the community can ask a resident to move out, how rate increases are handled, what happens to a deposit, and whether there is a community fee. The Consumer Voice guide above has a useful list of contract questions. If anything is unclear, an elder law attorney can review it in an hour.

Think about location the way your parent will

Families often choose the community closest to the adult child. That is reasonable, but ask your parent how they feel about it. A community a few minutes from Highway 153 is easy for the whole family to reach from across Chattanooga, and a resident who can still ride to a familiar drugstore or church keeps a piece of independence that matters.

Ask about transportation: which appointments are covered, how often, and whether a family member can ride along.

Do not wait for a crisis if you can help it

Here is the part most families do not know. Senior housing occupancy across the country passed 90 percent in the third quarter of 2026, the highest level since 2007, according to data from NIC. New construction has slowed, so the apartment you want may not be open on the day you need it. Touring a few months early costs nothing and gives your parent a say in the choice.

A short version to take with you

  • Confirm the state license and ask whether a secure dementia unit is approved, if that matters to you.
  • Ask whether memory care is offered, and get a plain answer.
  • Get the complete fee schedule in writing, and ask what causes the rate to change.
  • Ask how the community is paid and confirm your own plan with a benefits counselor.
  • Tour twice, once unannounced near a mealtime.
  • Ask for staffing ratios by shift and nurse hours.
  • Read the resident agreement at home before deciding.
  • Ask your parent what matters to them, and weigh it.
