Assisted Living, Nursing Home or Home Care: Which Fits?

Most families in Hixson and Chattanooga arrive at this question the same way.

November 20, 2026 · Regency House Assisted Living · 8 min read

Most families in Hixson and Chattanooga arrive at this question the same way. A parent falls, or stops cooking, or a hospital discharge planner says the words "she can't go home alone." Suddenly you are comparing three options you have never had to understand before: assisted living, a nursing home, and care at home. The names sound similar. The realities are very different.

This guide walks through what each option actually does, who it fits, what it tends to cost nationally, and who pays. It is written for the adult son or daughter doing the research, often late at night on a phone, who needs a clear picture before the next family conversation.

This article is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice.

The one-sentence version of each option

The National Institute on Aging describes the main long-term care settings this way, and the distinctions hold up well in practice:

  • Home care brings help to the house: a caregiver who comes for a set number of hours to assist with bathing, dressing, meals, errands and companionship. Nursing or therapy can be added through a home health agency when a doctor orders it.
  • Assisted living is a residence for people who need daily help but not round-the-clock medical care. Residents have their own apartment, eat in a dining room, and have staff on site at all hours for personal care, medication help and emergencies.
  • A nursing home, also called a skilled nursing facility, is a medical setting. Licensed nurses are on duty around the clock, and the building is built for people who need ongoing clinical care, complex medication management or rehabilitation after a hospital stay.

The deciding factor is almost never the building. It is how much help your parent needs each day, how predictable that need is, and whether it is medical or personal.

Home care: staying put, with help

Who it fits

Home care works best when a parent needs help for part of the day, when a spouse or adult child is nearby to cover the rest, and when the house itself is safe: few stairs, a walk-in shower, good lighting, neighbors who notice. It also suits people who are not ready to leave home and whose needs are still light.

Where it strains

Home care is priced by the hour, so the cost climbs in step with need. The CareScout 2025 Cost of Care Survey, released in March 2026, puts the national median for a non-medical in-home caregiver at about $35 an hour. Forty-four hours a week, which is what the survey calls a typical schedule, comes to roughly $80,000 a year, and that still leaves nights and weekends uncovered. Once a parent needs someone within earshot overnight, families often find that home care costs more than a residential option while providing less.

The other strain is the family. Coordinating caregivers, covering call-outs, managing medications from a distance and being the person who gets the 2 a.m. call is a second job. Many families in our area start with home care and move to assisted living not because it failed, but because it stopped being enough.

Assisted living: a home with a safety net

Who it fits

Assisted living is the middle ground, and for most older adults who can no longer live alone safely, it is the right one. A typical resident walks or uses a walker, manages with help on showers, dressing or medications, eats better when meals are social, and is safer with someone on duty at night. In Tennessee these communities are licensed by the Tennessee Health Facilities Commission as assisted-care living facilities, with rules that cover staffing, medication handling and resident rights.

Regency House in Hixson is this kind of community: 48 private apartments, three meals a day in the dining room, help with personal care and medications, housekeeping and laundry, a daily activity calendar, and staff present around the clock. It is a home first, with care built into it.

What it costs and who pays

The CareScout survey puts the national median for assisted living at about $6,200 a month. Rates vary by state, by community and by apartment size, and pricing models differ: some communities charge a base rent plus care tiers that rise over time, while others quote a single inclusive rate. Regency House uses one inclusive monthly rate set by the apartment, with no care levels or tiers; the rate is quoted by phone, and the rates and floor plans page explains what it covers.

Nationally, most assisted living is paid privately, from income, savings, a pension or the proceeds of a home sale. Regency House is a private pay community and also accepts participants in PACE, the Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly, whose care is coordinated and paid through the PACE organization. Our guide to paying for senior living walks through the options Hamilton County families use.

Where it strains

Assisted living is not a hospital and does not try to be one. A resident who needs a licensed nurse at the bedside every day, a feeding tube, a ventilator, or wound care that cannot be handled by an outside home health visit has outgrown the setting. Families whose parent needs a secure, locked memory care environment because of wandering should look for a community specifically licensed for that; standard assisted living is not designed for it.

Nursing home: medical care around the clock

Who it fits

A nursing home is the right place when the primary need is medical. That includes people recovering from surgery, a stroke or a serious illness who need daily physical, occupational or speech therapy, and people whose chronic conditions require licensed nursing every shift. The National Institute on Aging notes that nursing homes focus more on medical care than assisted living and offer rehabilitation services on site.

Two very different stays

It helps to separate short-term rehabilitation from long-term residence, because the money works differently. According to Medicare.gov, Medicare Part A can cover a short skilled nursing facility stay, up to 100 days per benefit period, after a qualifying hospital stay and only when the person needs daily skilled care. Medicare does not pay for custodial care, meaning help with bathing, dressing and eating, when that is the only care needed. So a parent may go from the hospital to a nursing home for rehab under Medicare, then face a choice when the skilled need ends and coverage stops.

Long-term nursing home residence is the most expensive option. The CareScout survey puts the national median for a semi-private room at roughly $9,600 a month, about $115,000 a year. Families who cannot pay that privately often turn to Medicaid, which in Tennessee is TennCare; eligibility rules are strict and vary by situation, so talk to a benefits counselor or an elder law attorney before counting on it.

How the three compare at a glance

  • Daily help with personal care: home care (during scheduled hours), assisted living (all day, every day), nursing home (all day, every day).
  • Someone on site overnight: home care only if you pay for it, assisted living yes, nursing home yes.
  • Licensed nurses on every shift: home care no, assisted living generally no, nursing home yes.
  • Private apartment with own door and furniture: home care yes (it is their house), assisted living yes, nursing home usually a shared or private room.
  • Meals, housekeeping and activities included: home care no, assisted living yes, nursing home yes.
  • Typical payer: home care private pay, assisted living private pay (with PACE at some communities, including ours), nursing home Medicare for short rehab and private pay or Medicaid for long stays.

Questions that point you to the right level

Families tell us the decision got easier once they answered these honestly, ideally with the parent's doctor in the conversation:

  • Does my parent need help every day, or only some days?
  • Is the help personal (showers, meals, medication reminders) or medical (injections, wound care, daily therapy)?
  • Has there been a fall, a missed medication or a hospital visit in the last six months?
  • Is anyone realistically available overnight, every night?
  • Is my parent eating well and seeing people, or isolated?
  • Is the house safe for someone with a walker, or would a single level with a walk-in shower change everything?
  • If we add up what we are spending on home care, medical alert, meal delivery and our own time off work, what is the real monthly number?

If the answers are mostly "every day," "personal," "nobody overnight" and "isolated," assisted living is usually the fit. If the answers are mostly "medical" and "daily therapy," start with a nursing home or rehab stay and reassess when the skilled need ends. If the answers are mostly "some days" and "someone is here," home care may carry you for a while, with assisted living as the plan for when it no longer does.

A note on hospital discharges

Many families meet this decision in a hospital hallway with a day to decide. Discharge planners in Chattanooga see this every week and can tell you quickly whether your parent's needs are skilled or custodial. If they are custodial, an assisted living community can often arrange a move within days, and a short respite stay lets you try the setting before committing. Ask the planner directly: "Does my mother need a nurse every day, or does she need help and supervision?" That one question sorts most cases.
