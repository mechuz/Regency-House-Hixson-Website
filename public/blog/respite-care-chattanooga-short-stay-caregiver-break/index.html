Respite Care in Chattanooga: A Real Break for Caregivers

If you are the one who checks on Mom every morning, fills Dad's pill organizer on Sundays and cancels your own plans when something goes wrong, you already know what caregiving costs.

November 17, 2026 · Regency House Assisted Living · 9 min read

If you are the one who checks on Mom every morning, fills Dad's pill organizer on Sundays and cancels your own plans when something goes wrong, you already know what caregiving costs. What many Chattanooga families do not know is that there is a name for the break you need, and a practical way to take it. It is called respite care, and a short stay in an assisted living community is one of the most common ways to arrange it.

This guide explains what respite care is, who it helps, how a short stay works at a community like Regency House in Hixson, what it generally costs across the market, and what help exists in Hamilton County for families who need it. It is written for the adult son or daughter doing the research late at night, so it stays plain and practical.

This article is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice.

What respite care actually means

Respite care is short-term care for an older adult so that the person who usually looks after them can rest, travel, recover from their own illness or simply catch up on life. The National Council on Aging describes it as any arrangement that gives a family caregiver a meaningful break, whether that is a few hours of help at home, a day program, or an overnight stay somewhere safe and staffed.

In an assisted living setting, respite usually means a furnished apartment for a set period, often a week or two and sometimes a month, with the same meals, housekeeping, help with daily routines and 24-hour staff presence that long-term residents receive. The person moves in with a suitcase, lives as a resident for the agreed time, and goes home when the stay ends. Nothing about it commits a family to a permanent move.

Why the break matters more than caregivers admit

Family caregiving in the United States has grown faster than most people realize. The Caregiving in the U.S. 2025 report from AARP and the National Alliance for Caregiving, released July 24, 2025, counts 63 million family caregivers nationally, a rise of about 45 percent in ten years. Nearly one in four of them provides 40 or more hours of care a week, and one in five rates their own health as fair or poor because of caregiving.

Those numbers describe a lot of people in Hixson, Red Bank, Soddy-Daisy and Signal Mountain who are running a second full-time job. When the caregiver gets sick, has surgery, loses sleep for weeks or quietly burns out, the parent's safety suffers too. A respite stay is not a luxury. It is maintenance for the whole arrangement that keeps a parent at home.

Common moments when families use respite

  • The caregiver's own medical event. A knee replacement, a cancer treatment cycle or a hospital stay leaves nobody at home to help.
  • Travel. A wedding out of state, a long-planned trip, or a work commitment that cannot be moved.
  • After a parent's hospital or rehab discharge. Someone who is not quite ready to be alone at home may do well with a few weeks of meals, medication reminders and people nearby. Families often arrange this directly with the hospital's discharge planner.
  • Home repairs or a move. A flooded kitchen, a new roof, or the weeks between selling one house and settling into another.
  • Exhaustion. Sometimes the honest reason is that the caregiver needs two weeks of uninterrupted sleep, and that is reason enough.

How a short stay works

Every community handles the details a little differently, so ask, but the general pattern in the Chattanooga area looks like this.

  1. A call and a conversation. You describe your parent's routine, what they can do on their own and where they need a hand. The community tells you whether a furnished apartment is open for your dates.
  2. A short assessment. A nurse or care coordinator meets your parent, in person or by phone, and gathers a current medication list and the doctor's contact information. The community needs to know what care your parent requires before move-in day, so this step protects your parent.
  3. Paperwork. A short-stay agreement, emergency contacts, physician orders for medications, and a list of what you are bringing.
  4. Move-in day. Most respite apartments come furnished, so the suitcase holds clothes, toiletries, a favorite blanket, photos and anything that makes the room feel familiar.
  5. The stay. Your parent eats in the dining room, joins activities if they wish, gets help with bathing or dressing as agreed, and has staff available around the clock.
  6. Going home. At the end of the agreed period your parent returns home. Many families use what they learned during the stay to adjust the care plan at home, and some decide a longer move makes sense. Either outcome is fine.

What to pack for a respite stay

  • Two weeks of comfortable clothing, plus a sweater for cool dining rooms
  • Non-slip shoes and slippers
  • All current medications in their original containers, with the pharmacy's name
  • Glasses, hearing aids with spare batteries, dentures and their cases
  • Walker, cane or other mobility aid your parent uses at home
  • A few photos, a favorite pillow or blanket, and reading material
  • A charged phone with family numbers saved, and the charger
  • A copy of the insurance card and any advance directive

What respite care costs, in general terms

Short stays in assisted living are usually priced by the day or the week and reflect the community's regular monthly rate. Nationally, the CareScout Cost of Care Survey, released in March 2026 from rates collected in late 2025, puts the median monthly cost of an assisted living community at about $6,200, with wide differences between states and between individual communities. Daily respite rates are commonly a bit higher per day than the monthly rate would suggest, because the community is holding a furnished apartment for a short period.

Regency House quotes one inclusive monthly rate by phone rather than publishing a menu of care levels and add-on fees. You can read how that works on the rates and floor plans page, and a short call will give you a clear number for the dates you need.

How families pay for a respite stay

Most families pay for respite privately, from the parent's savings or income or by pooling money among siblings. Regency House is a private pay community that also welcomes participants in PACE, the Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly. For a PACE participant, care is coordinated and paid through the PACE program, and the family should talk with their PACE team before arranging any stay. More on that is on our paying for senior living page.

It helps to know what the big public programs do and do not cover, because the internet is full of half-answers.

  • Medicare. Medicare does not pay for an assisted living stay, including respite. The one respite benefit it does include is for people enrolled in hospice: Medicare's hospice benefit covers up to five days at a time of inpatient respite in a Medicare-approved facility such as a hospice inpatient unit, hospital or nursing home, arranged by the hospice team. That is a different setting from an assisted living community.
  • Long-term care insurance. Some policies include a respite benefit with its own daily limit and waiting period. Only the policy and the insurer can tell you what yours covers, so call the number on the policy and ask for the respite provision in writing.
  • Veterans benefits. The Department of Veterans Affairs offers respite programs for eligible veterans through its own channels. Eligibility and settings vary, so a conversation with a VA social worker or a county veterans service officer is the right first step.

Regency House does not bill Medicare, Medicaid, TennCare, the VA or long-term care insurers directly. If you are counting on one of those programs, confirm the details with a benefits counselor before you book a stay anywhere.

Respite help in Hamilton County

Tennessee runs the National Family Caregiver Support Program through its Area Agencies on Aging and Disability. Hamilton County is served by the Southeast Tennessee Area Agency on Aging and Disability in Chattanooga. Depending on funding and eligibility, the program can offer caregiver counseling, support groups, training and, in some cases, respite assistance. The state also partners with the Tennessee Caregiver Coalition on a voucher program that helps some family caregivers pay for low-cost or no-cost respite. Availability changes with funding, so a call to the Area Agency on Aging is the only way to learn what is open to your family this year.

Two other local avenues are worth a mention. Hospital discharge planners at the Chattanooga-area health systems arrange short stays every week and can tell you what has worked for other families. And the Alzheimer's Association's Tennessee chapter runs caregiver support groups where respite comes up constantly; even if your parent does not have dementia, those rooms are full of people who have figured out how to take a break.

Questions to ask any community before a short stay

  • Is the apartment furnished, and what exactly is included in the daily or weekly rate?
  • What is the minimum and maximum length of a respite stay?
  • Who handles medications, and how do you want them delivered on move-in day?
  • Is a nurse on site or on call, and what happens if my parent needs more help than expected?
  • Can my parent join the activity calendar and dining room from the first day?
  • How do you handle a fall, a sudden illness or a trip to the emergency room, and who do you call first?
  • What is the cancellation policy if our plans change?
  • Can we visit any time, and can my parent keep their phone and routines?

Write the answers down. Comparing two or three communities on the same questions is far easier than comparing brochures.

A word about guilt

Many caregivers delay respite for months because arranging a break feels like admitting failure. It is the opposite. Caregivers who rest make fewer mistakes with medications, lose their temper less, notice changes in a parent sooner, and last longer in the role. A two-week stay that leaves you rested is one of the kindest things you can do for the person you care for.

Parents often surprise their children, too. People who swore they would hate "a place like that" come home talking about the lunch companions, the music hour or the fact that someone else did the laundry.
