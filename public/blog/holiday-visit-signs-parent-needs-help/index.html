Home for the Holidays: Signs a Parent Needs More Help

For many families in Hixson and around Chattanooga, Thanksgiving is the first long visit with Mom or Dad since summer.

November 15, 2026 · Regency House Assisted Living · 9 min read

For many families in Hixson and around Chattanooga, Thanksgiving is the first long visit with Mom or Dad since summer. You walk in, hug, and within an hour you notice things a phone call never showed you: the fridge is half empty, the mail is piled up, the stairs seem harder than they were. That quiet "something is different" feeling is one of the most common reasons adult children start researching help for a parent in December.

This guide is for that moment. It walks through what to look for during a holiday visit, how to tell ordinary aging from changes that deserve a doctor's attention, how to raise the subject without starting a fight, and what to do next if your parent needs more support than they have at home.

This article is general information, not medical, legal or financial advice.

Why the holidays reveal so much

Geriatric care managers and the Alzheimer's Association both point out the same thing: relatives who live apart only see each other a few times a year, so a holiday visit compresses months of gradual change into a single afternoon. A parent can sound fine on a ten-minute call. It is much harder to hide a dented car, an unfilled prescription or a kitchen that has not been used in days from someone staying in the guest room.

That makes the visit valuable, but it also means you are seeing a snapshot. Holidays are stressful and tiring for older adults too. Before you draw conclusions, the advice from aging specialists is simple: make observations, not assumptions, and write down what you actually saw so you can compare it with the next visit or share it with a doctor.

What to notice: a room-by-room walk

You do not need a clipboard. Just pay attention as you move through the house and the day. These are the areas care professionals look at first.

The kitchen and the fridge

  • Is there fresh food, or mostly expired items and single-serving snacks?
  • Are pots scorched, or is the stove untouched? Both can be signs: one of forgetfulness, the other of not cooking at all.
  • Has your parent lost noticeable weight? Clothes that hang loose or a belt on a new notch are easy to spot at the table.

The mail and the money

  • Unopened bills, past-due notices or duplicate payments suggest that managing finances has become harder.
  • Stacks of sweepstakes letters, unfamiliar charities or new "friends" who call about money are warning signs of scams. Older adults are a frequent target, and embarrassment keeps many from mentioning it.

The bathroom and the medicine cabinet

  • Pill bottles that are all full, all empty or long expired point to missed or doubled doses.
  • Look for grab bars, a non-slip mat and a night light. If they are missing, that is a fixable risk rather than a crisis.
  • Changes in hygiene, such as unwashed hair, the same clothes for days or a strong odor in the home, are often one of the first things families notice and one of the hardest to mention.

The parent

  • Movement: Is Dad holding onto furniture as he walks? Does getting out of a chair take two tries? Are there new bruises he cannot explain?
  • Mood: Has a social, talkative parent gone quiet, stopped going to church or the senior center, or lost interest in things she loved?
  • Driving: Ride along on an errand. Watch for drifting, slow reactions, missed signs and new dents on the car or garage door.

Falls: the risk families underestimate

If you only track one thing, track steadiness. According to the Centers for Disease Control and Prevention, more than 14 million adults age 65 and older, roughly one in four, report falling each year, and about 37 percent of those who fall need medical treatment for an injury. The CDC's data also show the fall death rate among older adults rose 21 percent between 2018 and 2024, with the sharpest increases among people 85 and older.

A fall is rarely "just a fall." It is often the event that turns a manageable situation at home into a hospital stay, a rehab stay and a hurried decision about where a parent will live next. Noticing a shuffle, a near-miss on the stairs or a reluctance to shower because the tub feels unsafe gives you time to act before that happens.

Normal aging or something more?

Every family worries about memory, and holiday visits are when memory changes are most often first noticed. The Alzheimer's Association draws a useful line. Occasionally forgetting a name or an appointment and remembering it later is a typical age-related change. Signs that deserve a medical evaluation include:

  • Forgetting recently learned information, or asking the same question repeatedly
  • Trouble following a familiar recipe or keeping track of monthly bills
  • Confusion about the date, the season or where they are
  • Misplacing things in odd places and being unable to retrace steps
  • Withdrawing from hobbies and social activities
  • Changes in mood or personality, such as becoming suspicious, fearful or easily upset

The Association is clear that serious memory problems are not a normal part of aging, and that many causes of confusion, including medication interactions, infections, thyroid problems and depression, are treatable. The right first step is a visit with your parent's primary care doctor, not a diagnosis at the dinner table.

One important note for families in our area: Regency House is an assisted living community and does not offer memory care or a secure dementia unit. If a doctor finds that your parent needs a secure memory care setting, look for a community specifically licensed for that level of care.

How to bring it up without a blow-up

Most parents do not refuse help because they are stubborn. They refuse because help feels like the first step toward losing independence, privacy and the home they built. Geriatric specialists suggest a few approaches that tend to go better than a family intervention over pie.

  1. Get siblings on the same page first. Disagree privately, not in front of your parent. A united, calm message is far easier to hear.
  2. Start with their worries, not yours. "What's the hardest part of the house these days?" opens a door that "You can't live here anymore" slams shut.
  3. Be specific and small. "Let's get a grab bar in the shower this week" is easier to accept than "You need help."
  4. Use the doctor as an ally. Many parents will accept a recommendation from their physician that they would reject from a child. Ask to join the next appointment.
  5. Expect several conversations. The goal of the first talk is for your parent to feel respected and heard, not to settle anything.
  6. Protect their dignity. Ask permission before looking through bills or the medicine cabinet, and explain why you are asking.
The most useful question is often not "Should Mom move?" but "What would have to be true for Mom to be safe and happy where she is, and how long can we make that true?"

A simple plan for the drive home

Before you leave, turn what you noticed into a short list. Families who do this find the following weeks far less chaotic.

  • Write down the specific things you observed, with dates. Avoid labels like "declining"; stick to facts like "three expired prescriptions" or "held the wall in the hallway."
  • Schedule a doctor's visit if you saw memory changes, weight loss, falls or mood changes, and ask whether a medication review is due.
  • Fix the easy safety items now: grab bars, brighter bulbs, remove loose rugs, add a night light on the path to the bathroom.
  • Call the Eldercare Locator, a free service of the U.S. Administration for Community Living, to find the Southeast Tennessee Area Agency on Aging and Disability, which serves Hamilton County and coordinates meals, transportation and in-home help.
  • Make sure someone has legal authority to help if needed. A durable power of attorney and a health care agent are far easier to arrange while a parent is well; an elder law attorney can explain Tennessee's requirements.
  • Learn what the options cost before you need them, so a future decision is informed rather than rushed.

When home is no longer enough

Sometimes the list on the drive home is short and solvable. Sometimes it is long, and the honest conclusion is that a parent is lonely, unsafe or exhausted by the work of running a house alone. In the Chattanooga area, families in that position usually weigh three paths.

More help at home

Home care agencies can provide a few hours a day of help with bathing, meals and errands. It works well when needs are light and predictable. It becomes expensive and hard to staff when a parent needs help overnight or around the clock, and it does not solve isolation.

A short respite stay

A respite stay is a furnished apartment in an assisted living community for a few weeks, with meals, housekeeping and help with daily tasks included. Families use it to recover after a hospital stay, to cover a caregiver's vacation or illness, or simply as a low-pressure trial. Many parents who would never agree to "move" will agree to "try it for a couple of weeks," and the experience often answers the question for everyone. You can read how respite works at Regency House on our respite care page.

Assisted living

Assisted living is a private apartment with staff available around the clock, three meals a day, housekeeping, activities and help with the parts of the day that have become hard, such as medication reminders, bathing and dressing. It is not a nursing home; residents keep their own furniture, their own routines and their front door key. In Tennessee, assisted living communities are licensed and inspected by the state, and you can ask any community for its most recent survey.

On cost, be wary of websites that quote a single national figure. Rates vary by city, apartment size and what is included. Regency House quotes one inclusive monthly rate by phone, with no care levels or tiered pricing, and the community is private pay with PACE accepted. If your parent is enrolled in the Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly, their care is coordinated and paid through PACE. Our paying for senior living guide explains how private pay and PACE work, and how other programs such as Medicare, TennCare and VA benefits work in general; confirm any benefit with a benefits counselor before you count on it.

You noticed for a reason

If you are reading this late at night after a holiday visit, trust what you saw. Most families wait a year longer than they should, not because they do not care, but because the next step feels enormous. It does not have to be. A doctor's appointment, a grab bar and one honest conversation are all real progress.
