Paying for care

IRS raises the 2027 limits on deductible long-term care insurance premiums; the cap for people over 70 is $6,290

American Association for Long-Term Care Insurance · Published November 10, 2026 · Shared November 20, 2026

The IRS has published its 2027 inflation adjustments, and the American Association for Long-Term Care Insurance summarizes what they mean for policyholders. The amount of a qualified long-term care insurance premium that can count as a medical expense rises by age band, topping out at $6,290 a year for people over 70, up from $6,200 in 2026; the cap is $5,030 for ages 61 to 70. The per-day benefit that can be received tax-free rises to $445. The association notes that the deduction only helps people who itemize and whose medical expenses clear the usual threshold, and that many hybrid life policies with care riders do not qualify.

Why it matters: If a parent pays long-term care premiums, the 2027 numbers belong in next year's tax planning; the policy's own benefit terms still decide what it pays toward assisted living.

McKnight's Senior Living · Published November 16, 2026 · Shared November 20, 2026

McKnight's Senior Living reports on a new survey of direct care employers showing that the share of unfilled healthcare positions fell to about 14 percent between 2024 and 2026, helped by recruiting and retention programs adopted after the pandemic. Why it matters: Staffing is the question behind every tour; ask how long the caregivers you meet have been there and how open shifts are covered.

Health & research

CDC: adults 75 and older should get a single RSV vaccine dose, and it does not need to be repeated each year

Centers for Disease Control and Prevention · Shared November 20, 2026

The CDC's adult guidance recommends one dose of RSV vaccine for everyone 75 and older and for adults 50 to 74 who are at higher risk of severe illness, a group that includes people with chronic heart or lung disease, diabetes, kidney disease and those living in nursing homes or other group settings. Unlike the flu shot, the RSV vaccine is not an annual vaccine, so someone who has already had a dose does not need another this season. RSV sends tens of thousands of older adults to the hospital each winter, and the CDC says the best time to get the shot is late summer or early fall, though it can be given any time during the season.

Why it matters: Check whether a parent has ever had the RSV shot; if not, it is a one-time errand worth asking the pharmacy or doctor about before the holidays.

Senior living demand keeps outrunning new construction; a new Argentum and NIC MAP report points to costs and margins

Senior Housing News · Published November 17, 2026 · Shared November 19, 2026

Senior Housing News reports on a new analysis from Argentum and NIC MAP that tries to explain why so few senior living communities are being built even as occupancy climbs. Construction starts fell to about 10,000 units in 2025 while demand kept rising. The report's authors argue that the industry measures demand well, through occupancy, move-ins and rates, but has little shared data on what communities spend and why operating costs have changed since 2020, and that thin margins rather than weak demand are what keep new projects from getting financed. The analysis is meant to give operators and lenders a common picture of expenses.

Why it matters: Few new communities are opening, so a family that finds a good fit should expect waiting lists and start the conversation early rather than wait for a new building.

Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services · Published November 17, 2026 · Shared November 19, 2026

The Centers for Medicare & Medicaid Services announced on November 17 that the standard Medicare Part B premium will be $209.40 a month in 2027, up $6.50 from $202.90 this year, and that the annual Part B deductible rises from $283 to $292. Why it matters: For a parent on a fixed income, the net January check is the number to budget care around; the December notice shows it.

AARP's checklist for judging whether an older parent needs more help at home

AARP · Shared November 19, 2026

AARP's caregiving guide lists the signs relatives can check during a visit when they wonder whether a parent is still managing alone. It covers changes in appearance and hygiene, unexplained weight loss, bruises or burns that may come from struggling with cookware, expired food in the refrigerator, unopened mail and late bills, new dents on the car, missed or unfilled prescriptions, and pulling back from friends and hobbies. Why it matters: Thanksgiving is often the first long visit of the year; a shared checklist turns vague worry into specific things a family can act on together.

WTOC (Savannah) · Published November 10, 2026 · Shared November 17, 2026

Georgia's first Program of All-Inclusive Care for the Elderly center opened in Savannah on November 10 and is now enrolling, WTOC reports. PACE is a Medicare and Medicaid program for people 55 and older who qualify for a nursing home level of care but can live safely at home or in the community with support. A PACE team coordinates medical care, therapy, medications, transportation and day services under one plan, and the program pays for that care. Tennessee already has PACE programs, and Regency House accepts PACE participants.

Why it matters: PACE can cover the care side for a frail parent who qualifies, which changes the math for families comparing home, assisted living and nursing home options.

Health & research

Large trial finds a simple screening tool in the doctor's office reduces falls in older adults

University of Texas Medical Branch (JAMA Network Open study) · Published October 19, 2026 · Shared November 11, 2026

A randomized trial of nearly 12,000 adults aged 65 and older, published in JAMA Network Open, tested a fall-risk tool built into the electronic health record used by primary care clinics. Patients whose doctors used the tool had fewer falls and fewer fall-related injuries than those receiving usual care. The tool prompts clinicians to ask about recent falls, balance and medications, and to act on the answers. Falls remain the leading cause of injury for older adults, and the researchers say routine screening at ordinary visits is one of the cheapest ways to prevent them.

Why it matters: At a parent's next checkup, ask the doctor directly for a fall-risk screening; it is quick and this study shows it makes a difference.
